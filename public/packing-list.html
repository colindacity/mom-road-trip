Packing List: Southwest to Glacier Road Trip

21 days (May 15 – Jun 4, 2026) · Phoenix → Grand Canyon → Page → Moab → SLC → Jackson → Yellowstone → Glacier → Missoula · 2 travelers, 1 compact SUV

Climate Zones You'll Cross

Low Desert (Days 1) Phoenix: 100F / 73F
Blazing sun, bone-dry air
High Desert (Days 2-9) Grand Canyon, Page, Moab: 78-90F / 45-60F
Hot days, cool nights, intense UV
Mountain (Days 10-15) Jackson, Teton, Yellowstone: 60-70F / 30-40F
Afternoon storms, cold mornings
Northern Rockies (Days 16-21) Glacier NP: 55-65F / 35-42F
Rain, possible snow, wind
Layering is everything. You'll experience a 62-degree temperature swing (38F mountain nights to 100F Phoenix). Pack a versatile layering system instead of bulky single-purpose items. Everything below is sized for doing laundry every 5-6 days.

Clothing (Per Person)

Base Layers & Tops

  • 4 Lightweight moisture-wicking t-shirts — synthetic or merino, NOT cotton. Light colors for desert.
  • 2 Long-sleeve sun shirts (UPF 50+) — double as base layers. Wear in desert to block UV. essential
  • 1 Lightweight merino or synthetic base layer top — for Yellowstone/Glacier mornings (38-45F)
  • 1 Lightweight base layer bottoms — wear under pants on cold Glacier mornings

Mid Layers

  • 1 Fleece jacket or pullover (midweight) — primary warmth layer for mountains. Patagonia Better Sweater or similar. essential
  • 1 Lightweight down or synthetic puffy jacket — packs small, critical for Glacier evenings and 38F mornings essential

Outer Layers

  • 1 Waterproof rain jacket with hood — Glacier gets rain/snow in late May. Also wind layer. Gore-Tex or similar. essential
  • 1 Lightweight wind jacket — optional if rain jacket is light enough. Useful for Grand Canyon rim breezes.

Bottoms

  • 2 Lightweight hiking pants — convertible zip-offs work great for desert-to-mountain flexibility
  • 2 Comfortable travel pants or leggings — for driving days and casual wear
  • 1 Shorts — for Phoenix and hot Moab days
  • 1 Comfortable sleep pants

Underwear & Socks

  • 6 Underwear — moisture-wicking synthetic or merino. 6 gets you to each laundry stop.
  • 4 Hiking socks (wool blend) — merino wool regulates temp from desert to mountains, dries fast
  • 2 Lightweight liner socks — optional, for blister prevention on hikes
  • 1 Warm socks for sleeping — for cold mountain nights

Accessories

  • 1 Wide-brim sun hat (UPF rated) — non-negotiable for desert. Full brim protects ears and neck. essential
  • 1 Warm beanie or knit hat — Yellowstone/Glacier mornings
  • 1 Lightweight gloves — for 38F mornings at Old Faithful or Glacier. Thin fleece is fine.
  • 1 Buff/neck gaiter — sun protection in desert, warmth in mountains, dust filter on trails
  • 1 Sunglasses (polarized, UV400) essential
  • 1 Sunglasses retainer strap — wind at overlooks
  • 1 Light scarf or shawl (Mom) — restaurant AC, drafty evenings

Sleepwear & Extras

  • 1 Pajama set or comfortable sleepwear
  • 1 Swimsuit — hotel pools in Phoenix, possibly SLC and Jackson
  • 1 Nice casual outfit — for Jackson Town Square dinner or nicer restaurants

Footwear

  • 1 Supportive hiking shoes or trail runners — broken-in, ankle support preferred for uneven terrain. This is the #1 item for Mom's comfort and safety. essential
  • 1 Comfortable walking shoes or sneakers — for driving days, town exploring, restaurants
  • 1 Sandals or flip-flops — hotel rooms, pool, letting hiking shoes air out
Mom's footwear note: Trails at Horseshoe Bend, Delicate Arch viewpoint, and Avalanche Lake have loose gravel and uneven surfaces. Sturdy, well-broken-in shoes with good tread are critical. Break them in 2-3 weeks before the trip, not during it.

Health & Medical

Prescription Medications (Mom)

  • All current prescription medications — 25-day supply minimum (21 days + buffer). Keep in original labeled bottles. essential
  • Copies of all prescriptions — paper copy + photos on phone. Include doctor's name and phone.
  • Ask doctor about acetazolamide (Diamox) — for altitude sickness prevention. Start 1-2 days before reaching 7,000ft+ (Grand Canyon, Day 2). Especially important for 80-year-old at altitude.
  • List of all medications, allergies, and conditions — laminated card in wallet + copy in glove box
Altitude warning for Mom. Grand Canyon rim (7,000ft), Yellowstone (7,500ft), and Teton (6,200ft) are genuine altitude. Symptoms: headache, nausea, fatigue, shortness of breath. The CDC recommends people with heart/lung conditions consult their doctor before traveling above 8,000ft. Allow extra rest days and avoid alcohol for the first 48 hours at each new elevation. Hydrate aggressively — at least 3-4 liters of water per day at altitude.

First Aid & OTC Medications

  • Pain relievers (acetaminophen AND ibuprofen) — ibuprofen also helps with altitude headaches
  • Antacid tablets (Tums or Pepto)
  • Anti-diarrhea medication (Imodium)
  • Antihistamine (Benadryl or Zyrtec) — allergies + helps with sleep
  • Hydrocortisone cream (1%) — bug bites, rashes
  • Antibiotic ointment (Neosporin)
  • Adhesive bandages (assorted sizes)
  • Moleskin — for blisters. Apply at the first sign of a hot spot.
  • Medical tape and gauze pads
  • Tweezers — splinters, cactus spines (real concern in Arizona)
  • Nail clippers
  • Small scissors

Sun & Heat Protection

  • 2 Sunscreen SPF 50+ (mineral, broad-spectrum) — apply every 2 hours. Arizona UV index regularly hits "extreme." Bring two bottles for 21 days. essential
  • 2 SPF lip balm (SPF 30+) — lips burn fast at altitude and in desert wind
  • Aloe vera gel (small bottle) — for sunburn. Buy in Phoenix if you forget.
  • Electrolyte packets or tablets (Nuun, Liquid IV, etc.) — pack 30+. Critical in desert heat and at altitude. Both cause faster dehydration. essential
  • Cooling towel — wet and drape around neck in Phoenix/Moab heat
  • Portable spray bottle or mister — quick cool-down for Mom in desert stops
Heat safety rules for the desert days (Days 1-9): Hike before 10am or after 4pm only. Drink 1 liter of water per hour when active outdoors. When you've finished half your water, turn around. Watch Mom for confusion, dizziness, stopping sweating (heat stroke warning signs). If in doubt, get to AC immediately.

Altitude & Cold Protection

  • Hand warmers (4-6 pairs) — for Yellowstone/Glacier early mornings. Cheap insurance.
  • Vaseline or Aquaphor — dry mountain air cracks skin and lips
  • Saline nasal spray — altitude + desert = dry nasal passages. Prevents nosebleeds.
  • Eye drops (artificial tears) — dry air irritates eyes, especially for seniors

Mom-Specific Medical Items

  • Collapsible walking stick or trekking pole — invaluable for stability on uneven trail surfaces. Can check one in luggage or buy at REI/Walmart in Phoenix.
  • Knee brace or support sleeve — if she has any knee issues, trails have uneven terrain
  • Compression socks — for flights and long driving days. Reduces swelling and DVT risk.
  • Small cushion or lumbar support — for long drives (4+ hours some days)
  • Medicare/insurance card + travel insurance docs essential
  • Medical alert info — ICE contact in phone, written card in purse

Electronics

  • Phone + charger essential
  • Car phone mount — for navigation. Essential on unfamiliar mountain roads.
  • Car charger (dual USB) — keep both phones charged during drives
  • Portable battery pack (10,000+ mAh) — no outlets on trails. Charge phones on long hike days.
  • Camera + charger/extra battery — if beyond phone camera. Cold drains batteries faster.
  • Earbuds or headphones — audiobooks/podcasts for driving days
  • Small flashlight or headlamp — for early morning starts, walking to car at night, power outages in remote areas
  • Multi-port USB wall charger — one charger block for everything. Hotels often have limited outlets.
  • Download offline maps before leaving — Google Maps offline for: Page AZ, Monument Valley, Moab, Teton area, Yellowstone, Glacier. Cell service is spotty to nonexistent in parks. essential

Documents & Money

  • Driver's license essential
  • Passport (Mom — Canadian citizen crossing no borders, but carry for airline ID) essential
  • America the Beautiful Annual Pass ($80) — covers all 6 national parks. One pass covers the whole car. Pays for itself after 2 parks ($35 each). Buy at first park entrance or online. essential
  • Car rental confirmation
  • Hotel/Airbnb confirmations — print backup copies. Cell service unreliable in parks.
  • Flight confirmations (print copies for Mom)
  • Travel insurance policy — strongly recommended for 80-year-old. Confirm it covers: trip interruption, medical evacuation, pre-existing conditions.
  • Health insurance cards (both travelers)
  • Credit cards (2 different cards) — some small-town gas stations or laundromats are cash-only
  • Cash ($200-300) — for tips, laundromats (quarters!), small-town vendors, Navajo Nation vendors at Monument Valley
  • Roll of quarters ($10 worth) — some laundromats are quarters-only
  • Emergency contacts list (printed) — doctor, insurance, embassy (Mom), roadside assistance, family
  • Antelope Canyon tour reservation — must be pre-booked with Navajo tour operator
  • Arches NP timed entry reservation — required Apr-Oct. Book at recreation.gov.

Hiking & Outdoor Gear

Hydration & Nutrition

  • 2 Reusable water bottles (32oz each, per person) — insulated keeps water cold in desert. National parks have refill stations. essential
  • 1 Small hydration bladder or extra bottle for longer hikes — Avalanche Lake trail (Glacier) is 3.5mi each way
  • Trail snacks — nuts, dried fruit, granola bars, jerky. Buy in bulk at Phoenix Costco on Day 1.
  • Electrolyte packets — listed in medical but worth repeating. Carry on every hike.

Day Pack

  • 1 Lightweight daypack (20-25L) — carries water, snacks, layers, first aid, sunscreen for all hikes essential
  • 1 Packable rain cover for daypack — or use a trash bag liner for Glacier rain days

Bear Safety (Days 10-21: Teton, Yellowstone, Glacier)

Bear spray is mandatory gear in grizzly country. Both grizzly and black bears are active in Grand Teton, Yellowstone, and Glacier during late May and June. The NPS recommends every hiker carry bear spray.

You cannot fly with bear spray. TSA prohibits it in both carry-on and checked luggage. Buy or rent upon arrival in the region.

Where to get it:

  • Jackson, WY (Day 10): Bear Aware at 565 N Cache St. Rental: $16/2 days, max $28. Or buy at any outdoor shop in Jackson ($40-60).
  • Yellowstone: Bear Aware kiosks at Canyon Village and Old Faithful.
  • Glacier area (Day 16): Bear Spray Shack 24hr kiosks in Whitefish, Columbia Falls, East Glacier. Rentals from $10. Or Glacier Outfitters in West Glacier.

Recommended plan: Rent one canister each in Jackson on Day 10. Return at a Yellowstone drop-off. Rent again in Columbia Falls for Glacier days. Total cost: ~$50-60 for both rentals vs. $80-120 to buy two cans you can't take home.

  • 2 Bear spray canisters — one per person, rent/buy locally (see above). Practice with demo canister. essential
  • 2 Bear spray holsters — usually included with rental. Must be accessible on hip, NOT buried in pack.
  • Bear bell — optional, some hikers clip one to their pack. Making noise is the real prevention.

Other Hiking Gear

  • Trekking poles (1-2 collapsible) — huge help for Mom's stability. Can rent in Jackson or buy telescoping poles at Walmart/REI.
  • Binoculars — wildlife viewing in Teton (moose, bison), Yellowstone (wolves, bears, elk), Glacier
  • Small sit pad or foldable seat — for rest stops on trails and cold/wet benches at overlooks. Mom will appreciate this.
  • Gaiters (lightweight) — optional, keeps trail dust and pebbles out of shoes
  • Insect repellent — mosquitoes emerge in late May/June near water. Yellowstone and Glacier can be buggy.

Car & Road Trip Supplies

  • Windshield sun shade — Phoenix and Moab will cook the car interior. $10 at any gas station.
  • Paper towels roll + wet wipes
  • Trash bags (small kitchen bags)
  • Cooler bag (soft-sided, collapsible) — for drinks, snacks, and picnic lunches. Buy ice at gas stations. Saves money vs. eating out every meal.
  • Reusable grocery bags — for groceries and laundry hauling
  • Blanket (lightweight) — picnics, extra warmth, napping in the car
  • Microfiber towel — dries fast, multi-use. Wipe sweat, clean up spills, impromptu picnic mat.
  • Tire pressure gauge — altitude changes affect tire pressure. Check when moving between desert and mountains.
  • Jumper cables or portable jump starter — rental car, but check it has one. Remote areas have limited AAA response.
  • Windshield washer fluid top-off — bugs on desert highways are relentless
  • Paper maps of national parks — free at visitor centers. Don't rely on phone in parks with no signal.

Toiletries & Personal Care

  • Toothbrush + toothpaste
  • Deodorant
  • Shampoo + conditioner — travel sizes or decanted. Hotels provide these but Airbnbs may not.
  • Body wash or soap
  • Face wash + moisturizer with SPF — desert air and altitude destroy skin hydration
  • Heavy moisturizer or lotion — apply at night. Cracking knuckles and dry patches are common.
  • Razor
  • Comb or brush
  • Tissues (travel packs, 4-5) — some trail toilets are out of TP
  • Hand sanitizer — trailhead restrooms are not always well-stocked
  • Laundry detergent pods (10-12) — cheaper than buying at the laundromat. Zip-lock bag them.
  • Dryer sheets (small pack)
  • Stain remover pen (Tide-to-Go)

Miscellaneous

  • Zip-lock bags (assorted sizes) — wet clothes, dirty laundry, organize small items, protect phone from rain
  • Packing cubes — sanity saver when living out of bags for 21 days. One for clean, one for dirty.
  • Carabiners (2-3) — clip water bottles, hats, shoes to daypack
  • Duct tape (small roll or wrap some around a water bottle) — fixes everything
  • Notebook + pen — Mom may enjoy journaling the trip
  • Playing cards or small travel game — for hotel evenings
  • Book or e-reader — for rest days and before bed
  • Dry bag or large zip-lock for phone — Horseshoe Bend, boat tours, rain in Glacier
  • Collapsible water bowl (if applicable) — skip if no dog
  • Small travel umbrella — for Glacier rain days and Sun Road stops where you can't always hike to shelter
  • Earplugs + eye mask — sunrise is early (5:30am) in June, thin hotel curtains

What NOT to Pack

  • Cotton t-shirts for hiking — cotton holds sweat, chafes, takes forever to dry. "Cotton kills" is the hiking mantra.
  • Jeans — heavy, slow to dry, uncomfortable for hiking and long drives. Bring hiking pants and travel pants instead.
  • More than 3 pairs of shoes — shoes eat car space fast. Three pairs (hike, walk, sandal) is the maximum.
  • Heavy coat or winter parka — layering system (base + fleece + puffy + rain shell) handles everything down to 30F without a bulky coat.
  • Full-size towels — hotels and Airbnbs provide them. A packable microfiber towel covers trail needs.
  • Bear spray from home — cannot fly with it (TSA bans from carry-on AND checked bags). Rent locally.
  • Excessive electronics (tablets, laptops, drones) — drones are banned in all national parks. Keep it simple.
  • Too many books — one book or an e-reader. You'll be too tired from the day to read much.
  • Formal clothes — Jackson is the fanciest stop and it's still casual-Western. No restaurant requires more than clean pants and a nice top.
  • More than 6 days of clothing — you're doing laundry every 5-6 days. Overpacking clothing is the #1 road trip mistake. A compact SUV with 2 travelers needs trunk space for cooler, daypack, and groceries.
  • Glass water bottles — weight, breakage risk. Use insulated stainless steel.
  • White clothing for trail days — red desert dust stains everything. Stick to earth tones and dark colors for hiking.

Special Notes

Glacier NP / Going-to-the-Sun Road (Days 16-20). The full road typically doesn't open until mid-to-late June. In late May/early June, expect the road to be open only from West Glacier to Avalanche Creek (about 16 miles in). Logan Pass will almost certainly still be closed. This is still spectacular — Lake McDonald and Avalanche Lake are stunning. But pack for rain, possible snow flurries, and temperatures in the 40s-50s at trail elevation. This is the coldest segment of the trip.
Mom's airport wheelchair assistance. Request wheelchair/mobility assistance at least 48 hours before each flight: YYZ→PHX outbound, MSO→connecting hub→YYZ return. Tip the wheelchair attendants $5-10 each. Confirm with airline on both outbound and return.
Packing strategy: two-bag system. For a 21-day trip, use one medium rolling suitcase (checked) + one personal daypack (carry-on). Keep one change of clothes, medications, and documents in the carry-on in case checked bags are delayed. In the car, keep an easily accessible "day bag" with sunscreen, water, snacks, layers, and first aid that goes on every outing.

Buy in Phoenix on Day 1

Stock up at a Phoenix Costco, Walmart, or Target after landing. These items are cheaper to buy locally than to pack or ship:
  • Case of water bottles — for the car cooler. Refill reusable bottles at parks.
  • Bulk trail snacks (nuts, bars, jerky, dried fruit)
  • Sunscreen (if you didn't pack enough)
  • Cooler bag (if you don't have one)
  • Windshield sun shade
  • Ice for the cooler — replenish at gas stations every 2-3 days
  • Fruit (apples, oranges — survive car heat)
  • Aloe vera gel
  • Any forgotten toiletries
