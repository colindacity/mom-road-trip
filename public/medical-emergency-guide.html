Key risk window: Grand Canyon (Day 3-4) and Yellowstone (Day 12-14). The Grand Canyon rim sits at 7,000 ft. She'll go from Phoenix (1,100 ft) to the rim in one day. Yellowstone averages 7,700 ft, with some areas above 8,000 ft. These are the two periods requiring the most altitude awareness.

Risk Factors for an 80-Year-Old

Counterintuitively, research suggests adults over 50 have slightly lower rates of acute mountain sickness (AMS) than younger people. However, there are specific concerns:

  • Pre-existing heart or lung conditions are the real risk multiplier, not age itself
  • Medications (especially diuretics) can worsen dehydration at altitude
  • Reduced cardiopulmonary reserve means less margin for error
  • AMS occurs in ~20% of people ascending to 7,000-9,000 ft
  • Research on elderly hikers shows healthy seniors acclimatize well, with performance normalizing after 5 days at altitude

Symptoms to Watch For

SeveritySymptomsAction
Mild AMS Headache, fatigue, dizziness, nausea, poor sleep, loss of appetite Rest, hydrate, take ibuprofen or acetaminophen for headache. Do not ascend further. Symptoms typically resolve in 1-3 days.
Moderate AMS Persistent vomiting, severe headache unresponsive to medication, increasing fatigue, shortness of breath with activity Descend at least 1,000-2,000 ft. If at Grand Canyon rim, drive to Flagstaff (7,000 ft down to 6,900, marginal) or further to Sedona (4,350 ft). If in Yellowstone, drive to Bozeman (4,820 ft) or Jackson (6,237 ft).
Severe (HACE/HAPE) Confusion, stumbling gait, severe breathlessness at rest, persistent cough, blue lips Medical emergency. Call 911 / park dispatch immediately. Descend immediately. These can be fatal within hours.

Prevention Strategies

  • Night in Flagstaff first (6,900 ft): The itinerary already has you stopping in Flagstaff before the Grand Canyon, which is excellent. This provides partial acclimatization.
  • Hydration: Drink 3-4 liters of water per day at altitude. Start extra hydration the day before ascending. Avoid alcohol for the first 48 hours at altitude.
  • Go slow the first day: Limit physical exertion the first day at the Grand Canyon rim. Easy walks only. Save longer hikes for Day 2 when somewhat acclimatized.
  • Sleep low: If possible, sleep at the lowest altitude you can. Tusayan (6,612 ft) is slightly lower than the rim (7,000 ft).
  • Eat light, carb-rich meals: Complex carbohydrates are easier to metabolize at altitude than fatty/heavy meals.
  • Monitor with pulse oximeter: Normal SpO2 at sea level is 95-100%. At 7,000 ft, 90-95% is expected. Below 88% warrants concern and possible descent.

Should She Ask About Acetazolamide (Diamox)?

Yes, discuss with her doctor. Here are the facts to bring up:
  • Standard prophylactic dose: 125 mg every 12 hours
  • Start the day before ascending to altitude, continue for 2-4 days at altitude
  • Works by stimulating breathing and speeding acclimatization from 3-5 days down to ~1 day
  • No geriatric-specific concerns per Mayo Clinic, BUT elderly patients are more likely to have kidney/liver issues that require dose adjustment
  • Side effects: tingling in fingers/toes, increased urination, carbonated drinks taste flat. Generally well-tolerated.
  • Contraindicated with sulfa allergy, severe kidney disease, or certain other conditions
  • At 7,000-7,700 ft, this is a moderate-risk elevation, not extreme. Her doctor may decide the benefit outweighs the risk, or may say it's unnecessary. Bring the elevation profile so the doctor has context.

Heat Exhaustion vs. Heat Stroke

Heat Exhaustion (treat immediately)
  • Heavy sweating (or absence of sweating in elderly)
  • Cool, clammy skin
  • Weak, rapid pulse
  • Nausea, dizziness, fatigue
  • Muscle cramps
  • Headache, fainting

Action: Move to shade/AC, remove excess clothing, apply cool wet cloths, sip water slowly. If no improvement in 30 minutes, seek medical help.

Heat Stroke (CALL 911 IMMEDIATELY)
  • Body temp 104 F+ (40 C+)
  • Hot, red, dry skin (no sweating)
  • Strong, rapid pulse
  • Confusion, slurred speech, disorientation
  • Loss of consciousness
  • Seizures

Action: Call 911. Move to cool area. Cool rapidly with ice packs on neck, armpits, groin. DO NOT give fluids if unconscious. This is a life-threatening emergency.

Prevention Protocol for Phoenix / Page / Moab

  • Schedule activities for early morning (before 10am) and late afternoon (after 4pm). Midday hours (11am-3pm) should be spent in air conditioning.
  • Hydration schedule: Drink 8 oz water every 30 minutes when outdoors, even if not thirsty. Aim for 3+ liters per day. Add electrolytes (Liquid IV, Nuun tablets) to at least one bottle per day.
  • Car strategy: Run AC 10 minutes before she gets in. Never leave medications in the car. Park in shade when possible. Keep a cooler with cold water in the back seat.
  • Cooling gear: Wet bandana for neck, wide-brim hat, UV-protective lightweight clothing. Carry a small spray bottle for misting face/neck.
  • Warning signs to verbalize: Ask her every 30 minutes outdoors: "How are you feeling? Any dizziness? Nauseous?" She may not volunteer symptoms.
  • Horseshoe Bend (Page) specific: The walk is 1.5 miles round trip with no shade and a steep return climb. Go at sunrise or skip if temps exceed 90 F. Bring 2 water bottles per person.
  • Antelope Canyon: Slot canyons are naturally cooler (shade), but the walk to/from the canyon entrance is exposed. Hydrate before going in.

5. Medications & Prescriptions

Crossing the Canada-US Border with Medications

Rules for Canadian residents entering the US:
  • Keep all medications in original, labeled pharmacy containers with her name on them
  • Carry a letter from her doctor listing all medications, dosages, and conditions
  • For controlled substances (opioids, benzodiazepines, ADHD meds): declare to the border officer. Carry no more than 90-day supply
  • Pack medications in carry-on luggage, never checked bags
  • Some Canadian OTC medications may be restricted in the US (e.g., codeine-containing products like Tylenol 1 are prescription-only in the US)
  • No special permits needed for standard prescriptions. The doctor's letter is a precaution, not a legal requirement, but it prevents problems

Medication Storage in Extreme Heat

Car interiors in Phoenix/Page/Moab can exceed 140 F. Most medications degrade above 86 F (30 C).

7. Travel Insurance (Canadian Traveling to the US)

Why OHIP Covers Almost Nothing

What OHIP pays (outside Canada)
  • ~$400 CAD/day for hospital stays
  • Nothing for physician fees
  • Nothing for ambulance
  • Nothing for air evacuation
  • Nothing for prescriptions
What US care actually costs
  • $10,000-30,000 USD/day for hospital
  • $3,500+ USD for ER visit
  • $20,000-80,000 USD for air ambulance
  • $50,000+ USD for ICU stay
  • Average senior travel claim: $40,000-100,000 USD
Travel medical insurance is non-negotiable. A helicopter evacuation from Yellowstone to Bozeman costs $20,000-50,000 USD alone. OHIP would cover about $400 of that. Without insurance, she'd be personally liable for the difference.

What the Policy Must Cover

  • Emergency medical treatment: Minimum $1,000,000 CAD (most good policies offer $2-5 million)
  • Emergency medical evacuation / air ambulance: Included in medical coverage
  • Repatriation to Canada: Transport home if medically necessary
  • Pre-existing condition coverage: She's 80; she'll likely have some pre-existing conditions. The policy MUST cover these or it's nearly useless. Most policies require conditions to be "stable" for 90-180 days before departure.
  • Trip cancellation / interruption: Covers non-refundable costs if she can't travel or must return early
  • Direct billing: The insurer pays the hospital directly rather than making her pay $50,000 upfront and reimburse later
  • 24/7 emergency assistance helpline: For coordinating care, finding doctors, arranging evacuation

8. Quick-Reference Emergency Protocols

If She Falls on a Trail

  1. Don't move her if she can't move on her own (possible fracture)
  2. Check for head injury: confusion, unequal pupils, loss of consciousness
  3. If she can stand: assess pain, swelling, range of motion
  4. If she cannot stand: call park dispatch (numbers in Section 10) or 911. Stay with her. Keep her warm (space blanket). Rangers can bring a litter.
  5. For ankle/knee injury: RICE (rest, ice, compress, elevate). Use ACE wrap from kit.

If She Shows Altitude Symptoms

  1. Check pulse oximeter. Below 88% SpO2 = descend.
  2. Mild headache/nausea: rest, hydrate, ibuprofen. Monitor for 2 hours.
  3. Getting worse (confusion, severe headache, vomiting): drive to lower elevation immediately.
  4. From Grand Canyon: drive to Flagstaff or further to Sedona/Phoenix
  5. From Yellowstone: exit toward Bozeman (north) or Jackson (south)

If She Shows Heat Illness Symptoms

  1. Get to shade or air-conditioned space immediately
  2. If conscious: cool water, electrolytes, wet cloths on neck/wrists/forehead
  3. Check temperature. Above 103 F = call 911
  4. If confused, slurring speech, not sweating, or unconscious: call 911 immediately. Apply ice to neck, armpits, groin. Do NOT give fluids if unconscious.

If She Has Chest Pain

  1. Call 911 immediately. Do not wait to see if it passes.
  2. Have her sit upright and stay calm
  3. If she takes nitroglycerin, help her take it as prescribed
  4. Give aspirin (325mg, chewed) unless she's allergic or her doctor said not to
  5. Be prepared to perform CPR if she loses consciousness
  6. In national parks: call park dispatch, they may have closer response than county EMS
